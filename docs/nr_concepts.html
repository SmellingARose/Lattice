1. The 3+1 Split (ADM)

Q: What are the lapse and shift, and what do they physically control?

The ADM formalism splits 4D spacetime into a stack of 3D spatial slices evolving in time. Two quantities control how the slices are laid out:

  • Lapse (α) — controls how much proper time elapses between adjacent slices at each point. A large lapse means time runs fast there; a small lapse means time is frozen.
  • Shift (βi) — controls how spatial coordinates slide sideways between slices. A nonzero shift means the coordinate labels drift relative to the physics.

Together they define the coordinate system. The physics doesn't depend on their values, but the stability and quality of a simulation depends critically on choosing them well.

Q: What is the extrinsic curvature Kij? What does it measure?

The extrinsic curvature measures how each spatial slice is embedded in 4D spacetime — specifically, how the slice bends. Formally:

Kij = −(1/2α) (∂t gij − Diβj − Djβi)

It is the "time derivative" of the spatial metric (up to gauge). A spatial slice through flat spacetime has Kij = 0. A slice through a collapsing region has large Kij. Together, gij and Kij are the 12 dynamical variables of general relativity.

2. Conformal Decomposition

Q: Why decompose the metric into a conformal factor and conformal metric?

Evolving the raw ADM equations (gij and Kij directly) is numerically unstable. Simulations blow up within ~10M of evolution. The reason is mathematical: the ADM system is only weakly hyperbolic, meaning small perturbations can grow without bound.

The fix (Shibata & Nakamura 1995, Baumgarte & Shapiro 1998): split the metric into two pieces:

gij = χ−1 h̃ij

where χ (chi) carries the "size" and h̃ij carries the "shape" with det(h̃) = 1. Similarly, Kij is split into its trace K and traceless part Ãij.

This separates gauge/constraint degrees of freedom from physical ones. The resulting system (BSSN) is strongly hyperbolic, which is required for numerical stability.

Evolving raw ADM is weakly hyperbolic. The conformal decomposition makes the system strongly hyperbolic, which is required for numerical stability.

Q: What is chi (χ)?

Chi is the conformal factor:

χ = det(g)−1/3

In flat space, χ = 1. Near a black hole singularity, χ → 0. The physical metric is recovered as gij = h̃ij / χ.

Some codes use ψ (psi) instead, where gij = ψ4 h̃ij. The problem is that ψ → ∞ at punctures, which is numerically troublesome. Chi goes to zero instead, which stays smooth and finite everywhere on the grid.

3. CCZ4 Formulation

Q: What is the difference between BSSN and CCZ4? What is Theta?

BSSN (the conformal decomposition described above) works, but has a weakness: constraint violations, once they appear numerically, just sit there and slowly grow. Nothing in the BSSN equations pushes them back toward zero.

CCZ4 (Alic et al. 2012) adds a new evolved scalar variable Θ and a vector Zi that directly encode constraint violations:

  • Θ ≈ Hamiltonian constraint violation
  • Zi ≈ momentum constraint violation

The key innovation: CCZ4 includes damping terms proportional to a parameter κ1 that exponentially drive Θ and Zi back to zero. When constraints are violated (as they always are in finite-precision numerics), CCZ4 actively cleans them up on a timescale ~1/κ1.

CCZ4 is BSSN with built-in constraint damping. It adds a scalar Θ that tracks constraint violations and exponentially damps them.

Q: What are the constraint equations, and why does violation matter?

Einstein's equations split into two types:

  • Evolution equations — how gij and Kij change in time (integrated with RK4).
  • Constraint equations — conditions that must hold on every time slice.

The Hamiltonian constraint:

R + K² − KijKij = 0

The momentum constraint:

DjKji − DiK = 0

These are analogous to ∇·E = ρ and ∇·B = 0 in electromagnetism: they must hold at all times, but you don't evolve them directly. If your numerics are perfect, they stay satisfied forever. In practice they drift, and the magnitude of constraint violation is the primary measure of code correctness. Convergence testing checks that violations shrink at the expected rate as resolution increases.

4. Gauge Conditions

Q: What gauge conditions does the code use, and why?

"Gauge" means the choice of lapse α and shift βi, which determine how you slice spacetime and how coordinates move.

1+log slicing (lapse):

∂tα = −2α(K − 2Θ)

This drives the lapse to collapse (α → ~0.3) near black holes, effectively freezing time at the singularity. The name comes from the analytic solution α = 1 + log(g) in certain cases.

Gamma-driver (shift):

∂tβi = (3/4) Bi
∂tBi = ∂tΓ̃i − η Bi

This makes coordinates "follow" the black holes as they move, preventing coordinate stretching. The damping parameter η controls how quickly the shift responds.

1+log slicing freezes the singularity by collapsing the lapse. The Gamma-driver shift lets coordinates track moving black holes.

Q: What is a "moving puncture" and why does gauge choice matter?

A puncture is a point where the black hole interior's spatial infinity is compactified into a single grid point. The metric is singular there (χ → 0), but with the right gauge, you can evolve right through it.

The breakthrough (Campanelli et al. 2005, Baker et al. 2005): with 1+log slicing + Gamma-driver shift, the singularity settles into a stable trumpet geometry. The lapse collapses at the puncture, freezing time evolution there. The shift keeps the puncture from drifting off the grid.

"Moving puncture" means the puncture is free to move across the grid as the shift vector pushes it. Before this discovery, groups tried to excise the black hole interior (cutting a hole in the grid), which was fragile and couldn't handle mergers.

Moving punctures let you evolve through black hole singularities by using gauge conditions that freeze the singular point into a stable trumpet slice.

5. Initial Data

Q: Why can't you freely specify all 12 variables for initial data?

You want to set gij (6 components) and Kij (6 components) at t = 0. But the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints must hold on the initial slice. That gives 4 equations constraining your 12 free functions.

So you freely choose 8 components (the "free data") and solve elliptic PDEs for the remaining 4. This is why the code includes a multigrid solver — it solves those constraint equations on the initial time slice.

The standard approach (conformal transverse-traceless decomposition): freely specify the conformal metric h̃ij and the traceless-transverse part of Ãij, then solve for χ (from the Hamiltonian constraint) and a vector potential Wi (from the momentum constraint).

Q: What is a puncture? What is Bowen-York data?

Puncture method (Brandt & Brügmann 1997): Write the conformal factor as ψ = ψBL + u, where

ψBL = 1 + Σ Mi / (2ri)

is the analytic Brill-Lindquist piece (encodes the mass of each black hole) and u is a smooth correction solved numerically. The equation for u is a single elliptic PDE — this is what the multigrid solver computes.

Bowen-York data: An analytic closed-form solution for the extrinsic curvature Ãij that gives black holes with specified linear momentum Pi and spin Si. It satisfies the momentum constraint exactly (analytically), so you only need to solve the Hamiltonian constraint for the conformal factor — reducing the problem to one elliptic PDE instead of four.

Bowen-York gives an analytic extrinsic curvature for black holes with arbitrary momentum and spin. You then solve one elliptic PDE for the conformal factor using multigrid.

6. Numerical Methods

Q: What does "6th-order finite differences" mean, and why not 2nd order?

Finite differences approximate derivatives on a discrete grid. A 2nd-order stencil uses 3 grid points and has error proportional to dx². A 6th-order stencil uses 7 grid points and has error proportional to dx6.

The payoff: when you double resolution (halve dx), 2nd-order error drops by 4×, but 6th-order error drops by 64×. This means 6th-order achieves the same accuracy at much coarser resolution, saving enormous amounts of memory and computation. For 3D grids where cost scales as N³, this is the difference between feasible and impossible.

Q: What is Kreiss-Oliger dissipation and why is it needed?

High-order finite differences are accurate but introduce spurious high-frequency oscillations (grid-scale noise) that can destabilize the simulation. Kreiss-Oliger (KO) dissipation adds a small amount of artificial viscosity that selectively damps only the highest-frequency modes — the ones the grid can't resolve properly.

The dissipation operator is one order higher than the finite difference scheme (7th-order for 6th-order FD), so it doesn't affect the convergence rate of the physical solution. The strength is controlled by a parameter σ (typically 0.3). Too little dissipation and noise grows; too much and you damp the physics.

Q: What does "4th-order convergence" mean and how do you test it?

If the code is correct, the error should decrease as a known power of the grid spacing: error ∝ dxp, where p is the convergence order.

To test: run the same problem at 3 resolutions (e.g., N = 32, 64, 128). Compute a diagnostic like the Hamiltonian constraint violation at each resolution. If the code is correct:

H(N=32) / H(N=64) ≈ 2p

For p = 4 (4th-order convergence), the coarse-resolution error should be ~16× larger than the fine-resolution error. In practice, the code achieves convergence order ~6.5 for most variables (from the 6th-order stencils), but some operations like boundary conditions limit the overall order to ~4 in certain regions.

This is the single most important test. Wrong convergence order means a code bug. Correct order but wrong magnitude means a physics issue.

7. AMR & Gravitational Waves

Q: Why do binary black hole simulations need adaptive mesh refinement?

Binary BH simulations face a severe scale separation problem. Near each puncture you need grid spacing dx < 0.1M to resolve the steep gradients in χ. But gravitational waves must be extracted at r > 100M, requiring a domain hundreds of M across.

A uniform grid covering both scales would need N ~ 2000+ per dimension — that's 8 billion grid points × 31 fields × 8 bytes ≈ 2 TB of memory, which is impractical.

AMR solves this by using fine grids only where needed (near the black holes) and coarse grids in the far field. A typical setup might use 6 refinement levels, with each level halving the grid spacing. This reduces memory from terabytes to tens of gigabytes while maintaining accuracy where it matters.

Q: What is Psi4 (Ψ4) and why do you need it?

Ψ4 is a complex scalar constructed from the Weyl curvature tensor — it encodes outgoing gravitational radiation. At large distances from the source:

Ψ4 ≈ d²h/dt²

where h is the gravitational wave strain that detectors like LIGO measure.

To extract it: decompose Ψ4 into spin-weighted spherical harmonics Yℓm on extraction spheres at various radii. The dominant mode (ℓ = 2, m = ±2) gives the quadrupolar radiation pattern. The amplitude encodes the energy being radiated; the phase encodes the orbital dynamics.

Without Ψ4, a numerical relativity code is a sandbox. With it, it becomes a tool for gravitational wave science.

8. The Full Pipeline

The complete numerical relativity pipeline, from mathematical formulation to physical observables:

4D Einstein equations
  ↓ 3+1 decomposition (ADM)
Spatial metric gij + extrinsic curvature Kij
  ↓ conformal decomposition (stability)
χ, h̃ij, K, Ãij, Γ̃i
  ↓ add constraint damping (CCZ4)
+ Θ, Zi with κ1 damping
  ↓ gauge choice
1+log lapse + Gamma-driver shift (moving punctures)
  ↓ initial data
Bowen-York Ãij + multigrid solve for χ
  ↓ time evolution
RK4 + 6th-order finite differences + KO dissipation
  ↓ wave extraction
Ψ4 decomposed into (ℓ, m) modes → gravitational waveforms
